How the coaching agent judges a swing

Our coaching agent turns phone video into one clear fix, a cue and a drill. Before it reaches players, we want coaches to check the logic: what we measure, the ranges we compare against, when we call something a problem, and the words players read.

How it works

The agent never sees the video. It receives numbers measured from each swing or pitch, and fixed rules decide what those numbers show before the agent writes a word.

  1. Measure every rep Video engineThe video engine tracks body points and measures each swing or pitch: stride, knee angles, trunk tilt, timing. Each number comes with a confidence level (high, medium or estimate).
  2. Compare with the typical range Fixed rulesEach rep is checked against the typical range for the player’s level. Estimates are never used as evidence.
  3. Look for a repeated pattern Fixed rulesA problem is only flagged when it shows up on several reps. One bad swing is not a pattern.
  4. Pick one priority fix AgentThe agent chooses the one problem that matters most from the ones the rules confirmed, and writes the explanation, a cue and one drill from our library.
  5. Check every word Fixed rulesEvery claim is checked against the numbers. If the note breaks a rule, the agent rewrites it. If it still fails, the player gets a fixed rule-based note instead.

The mechanics score (0–100) is calculated by fixed rules from the same numbers. The agent cannot see or change it.

What we measure, and the ranges

Ranges come from lab motion-capture data on real high school and college players, converted to what a phone camera measures. They describe what is typical for a level, not what is ideal.

Only measurements marked Judged can create a problem or count in the score. Measurements marked Shown only are reported to the player, but the camera cannot measure them reliably yet, so the agent never praises or criticises them.

Hitting is judged on two measurements today: stride length and body drift past the front foot. Timing, head movement, knee angle and hip-shoulder separation are shown only, until we can measure them reliably from a phone.

When a problem gets flagged

A problem appears in a player’s note only when every one of these is true:

  • The rep clearly misses the range. A rep only counts if it misses by more than a small margin: 10% of the range’s width. When the range is based on few athletes, the margin is bigger (1.5× for medium-confidence ranges, 2.5× for low-confidence ranges), so we don’t flag players against a rough range.
  • It shows up on at least 2 reps, and on at least 30% of the reps measured.
  • At least half the reps were measured with high or medium confidence. Estimates never count.

Example: high school hitting stride is 39.9–52.2% of height, a width of 12.3. That range is low confidence, so the margin is 10% × 2.5 × 12.3 = 3.1. A swing counts as overstriding past 55.3%. For college (medium confidence) the line is at 57.8%.

Major or minor

A problem is major when the typical miss is at least half a range width, it shows on at least 50% of reps, and at least 3 reps were measured. Otherwise it is minor. Major problems rank higher.

Arm slot works differently

Arm slot can’t be compared with a typical range, because a small change in camera angle shifts it by 8–20°. Instead we compare each pitch with the pitcher’s own median arm slot. A pitch counts when it is more than 9° away from that median, with at least 3 pitches measured. The usual 2-rep and 30% rules still apply.

Which problem comes first

When more than one problem is confirmed, the player gets one priority fix. The rules rank the problems, and the agent sees that ranking. The agent may pick a different one if it has a reason, and we track how often it does.

Each problem gets a priority number. Higher goes first:

FactorValues
How seriousMajor 2, minor 1
Part of the bodyLower body 1.5, trunk 1.2, upper body and head 1.0
Measurement confidenceHigh 1.0, medium 0.7
How well the range is establishedHigh 1.0, medium 0.85, low 0.5
How often it showedShare of reps, 0 to 1

The factors are multiplied. Lower body goes first because the legs set up everything above them.

Drills

The agent recommends exactly one drill, and only from this list, chosen from the drills listed for the priority fix. Rep schemes are sets × reps.

The mechanics score

The score answers one question: how close are this player’s judged measurements to the typical range for their level? It is not a rating of how good a player is. Players see that sentence next to the score.

  • Each rep: inside the range scores 100. Outside it, the score drops in a straight line and reaches 0 at two range widths outside.
  • Each measurement: the average over the reps that measured it.
  • Overall: a weighted average. Weights use the same body-part, confidence and range factors as the priority order.
  • No score if fewer than 2 judged measurements, or if the video could not be read.

Trust level is the average of measurement confidence × range confidence across the judged measurements.

All high school ranges are currently low confidence (based on 12 hitters and 7 pitchers), so every high school score is marked low confidence until we have more data.

What the agent may and may not say

Every note is checked before a player sees it.

Must

  • Name a problem only if the rules confirmed it, and point to the measurement that shows it.
  • Give one cue and one drill from the library when there is a fix.
  • Ask the player to record again when the video could not be read, and give no praise or fixes in that case.
  • Stay short: a headline of up to 25 words, up to 3 things that look solid, up to 2 things to work on, up to 3 notes about the data, and a cue of up to 20 words.
  • Read at about an 8th-grade level, in plain English.

Must not

  • Praise anything that is outside the range, is an estimate, or is evidence of a problem.
  • Use any number that isn’t in the player’s measurements.
  • Use judging words: good, bad, great, perfect, wrong, poor, weak, mistake.
  • Use medical words: injury, pain, sore, strain, tendon, ligament, UCL, doctor, surgery, health.
  • Include links, email addresses or phone numbers.

Questions for you

  3. Is 9° the right tolerance for arm slot from pitch to pitch?
  4. Is “2 reps and 30% of reps” the right bar for calling something a pattern?
  5. Should lower-body problems always come first?
  6. Hitting has no “stride too short” problem today. Should it?
  7. In example 2, a pitcher with two flagged problems still scores 96, “Very close to typical”. Does that feel right, or should flagged problems pull the score down more?
  8. Are the problem descriptions, cues and drills right for each problem? Is anything missing?
  9. Are 3×8 rep schemes right across the board?
